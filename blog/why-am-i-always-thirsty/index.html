Why Am I Always Thirsty? Causes and When to Worry

October 5, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Most constant thirst comes from ordinary fluid loss: sweat, salty or spicy food, or not drinking enough. Thirst that stays for days with no clear reason needs a check. The causes a doctor looks for first are high blood sugar from diabetes, a medicine side effect, and dry mouth that feels like thirst. MedlinePlus says to contact a health care provider when excessive thirst is ongoing and unexplained.

What counts as excessive thirst?

Thirst is the body's signal to replace lost water. MedlinePlus defines excessive thirst as an abnormal feeling of always needing to drink fluids. The medical name is polydipsia.

The same page calls excessive thirst a common symptom. It is often a reaction to fluid loss during exercise or to salty foods. In those cases, drinking until the thirst stops is the correct response.

The pattern that needs attention is different. You drink, the thirst comes back fast, and you can't link it to heat, exercise, or food. Often you also pass more urine than usual.

What are the common everyday causes of constant thirst?

MedlinePlus lists a recent salty or spicy meal first among the causes of excessive thirst. Fluid loss is the other everyday cause.

The MedlinePlus dehydration page lists the people who are at higher risk of running low on fluid:

  • People who exercise or work outdoors in hot weather.
  • People who take medicines that make them urinate or sweat more.
  • People with long-term illnesses that make them urinate or sweat more, such as diabetes or kidney problems.
  • Older adults. Some people lose their sense of thirst with age, so they drink too little.

Signs of dehydration on that page include feeling very thirsty, dark-colored urine, and urinating and sweating less than usual. If your urine is dark and you urinate less, you probably need more fluid. If you are thirsty but pass large amounts of pale urine, look at the causes below.

Could constant thirst be a sign of diabetes?

Yes. This is the cause to rule out first. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) lists feeling very thirsty and increased urination among the common symptoms of diabetes. Others include blurred vision, fatigue, and sores that do not heal.

The speed of onset differs by type. NIDDK says type 1 symptoms usually develop quickly, over a few days or weeks, and can include unexplained weight loss. Type 2 symptoms can develop slowly, over several years, and many people have no symptoms. If you also feel worn out, read our guide to the causes of fatigue. If you lose weight and do not try to, read when unintentional weight loss is a warning sign.

A blood test gives the answer. NIDDK publishes these diagnostic cutoffs for people who are not pregnant, with the American Diabetes Association as the source:

  • A1C: 6.5% or above means diabetes. 5.7% to 6.4% means prediabetes.
  • Fasting plasma glucose: 126 mg/dL or above means diabetes. 100 to 125 mg/dL means prediabetes.
  • Oral glucose tolerance test, at 2 hours: 200 mg/dL or above means diabetes. 140 to 199 mg/dL means prediabetes.
  • Random plasma glucose: 200 mg/dL or above means diabetes. Doctors use this test when a person has symptoms.

NIDDK adds that a doctor usually uses a second test to confirm diabetes. The same page says anyone who has symptoms of diabetes should be tested.

Can medicines or a dry mouth make you thirsty?

Yes to both. MedlinePlus names four medicine groups as causes of excessive thirst: anticholinergics, demeclocycline, diuretics, and phenothiazines. Diuretics are often called water pills.

Lithium is another one. The MedlinePlus lithium drug page lists mild thirst and dry mouth as side effects to report if they are severe or do not go away. It lists excessive thirst and frequent urination as serious. For those, the page says to call your doctor immediately or get emergency help.

Dry mouth is a separate problem that feels like thirst. The MedlinePlus dry mouth page says it means you do not have enough saliva to keep your mouth wet. Causes on that page include:

  • Side effects of some medicines for high blood pressure, depression, and bladder-control problems.
  • Diseases such as Sjogren's syndrome, HIV, salivary gland disorders, and diabetes.
  • Radiation therapy and chemotherapy.
  • Damage to the nerves that tell the salivary glands to make saliva.

Blood pressure medicines are on that list. Our sibling site hypertension.md covers them. Do not stop a prescribed medicine by yourself. MedlinePlus says a provider or dentist may adjust the dosage or switch medicines when a medicine is the cause.

What less common conditions cause constant thirst?

Diabetes insipidus. This disorder is not related to diabetes mellitus. Blood glucose is normal, but the kidneys can't concentrate urine. NIDDK says it is rare, affecting about 1 in 25,000 people worldwide. Most people make 1 to 3 quarts of urine a day. People with diabetes insipidus can make up to 20 quarts. The usual cause is a problem with the hormone vasopressin. Either the body makes too little of it, or the kidneys do not respond to it.

You may see a newer name. In 2022, an international working group proposed arginine vasopressin deficiency for the central form and arginine vasopressin resistance for the nephrogenic form. The NIDDK page still uses the older name.

High blood calcium. The MedlinePlus hypercalcemia page lists increased thirst and more frequent urination among the symptoms, due to changes in the kidneys. The most common cause is too much parathyroid hormone. Other causes on that page include some cancers, too much vitamin D, thiazide diuretics, and lithium. Most people with high calcium have no symptoms, and a routine blood test finds it.

Thirst that starts in the brain. MedlinePlus lists psychogenic polydipsia, which it describes as a mental disorder, among the causes. NIDDK describes a related type called dipsogenic diabetes insipidus. A problem with the hypothalamus makes a person feel thirsty and drink more. NIDDK warns that blood sodium can drop too low in this condition.

Serious fluid shifts. MedlinePlus also lists heavy bleeding, burns, severe infections (sepsis), and heart, liver, or kidney failure.

When should you see a doctor about thirst?

MedlinePlus says to contact your health care provider if:

  • Excessive thirst is ongoing and unexplained.
  • Thirst comes with other unexplained symptoms, such as blurry vision or fatigue.
  • You pass more than 5 quarts (4.75 liters) of urine a day.

Some signs need emergency care. NIDDK lists these symptoms of diabetic ketoacidosis, which it calls a medical emergency: feeling very tired, trouble breathing, fruity-smelling breath, fainting from dehydration, and abdominal pain, nausea, or vomiting.

The MedlinePlus dehydration page says to get medical help right away if dehydration comes with any of these:

  • Confusion
  • Fainting
  • Lack of urination
  • Rapid heartbeat
  • Rapid breathing
  • Shock

NIDDK lists feeling dizzy or light-headed when standing as a symptom of dehydration. Our article on feeling dizzy when you stand up covers that symptom.

What tests will a doctor order for excessive thirst?

The visit starts with questions. MedlinePlus lists what a provider may ask. Did the thirst start suddenly or slowly? Does it stay the same all day? Did you gain or lose weight without trying? Do you urinate more or less than usual?

Bring a list of every medicine and supplement you take. Calcium and vitamin D supplements matter here, because MedlinePlus links both to high blood calcium.

MedlinePlus lists these tests for excessive thirst:

  • Blood glucose level
  • Complete blood count (CBC) with white blood cell differential
  • Serum calcium
  • Serum sodium
  • Serum osmolality and urine osmolality
  • Urinalysis

If the doctor suspects diabetes insipidus, NIDDK says the workup can include a water deprivation test and an MRI of the brain. A water deprivation test needs medical supervision. Do not try to restrict fluids at home to test yourself.

The bottom line

Thirst after heat, exercise, or a salty meal is normal, and water fixes it. Thirst that stays for days with no clear cause needs a visit and a blood glucose test. Go sooner if you also pass large amounts of urine, lose weight, or have blurry vision. Confusion, fainting, trouble breathing, or fruity-smelling breath with thirst need emergency care.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is being thirsty all the time always a sign of diabetes?

No. MedlinePlus says excessive thirst is often a reaction to fluid loss during exercise or to salty foods. Medicines, dry mouth, high blood calcium, and diabetes insipidus are other causes. A blood glucose test shows whether diabetes is the cause.

What is the difference between thirst and dry mouth?

Thirst is the body's signal to replace lost water. Dry mouth means you do not have enough saliva to keep your mouth wet. MedlinePlus lists some medicines for blood pressure, depression, and bladder control, and diseases such as Sjogren's syndrome, as causes of dry mouth.

How much urine in a day is too much?

NIDDK says most people make 1 to 3 quarts of urine a day. MedlinePlus says to contact a health care provider if you pass more than 5 quarts (4.75 liters) a day.

Is diabetes insipidus a type of diabetes?

No. NIDDK says diabetes insipidus and diabetes mellitus are not related, although both can increase thirst and urination. In diabetes insipidus, blood glucose is normal but the kidneys cannot concentrate urine. A 2022 working group proposed the names arginine vasopressin deficiency and arginine vasopressin resistance.

Can a medicine make me thirsty?

Yes. MedlinePlus lists anticholinergics, demeclocycline, diuretics, and phenothiazines as causes of excessive thirst. Its lithium page says to call your doctor immediately for excessive thirst or frequent urination. Do not stop a prescribed medicine without your doctor's advice.
